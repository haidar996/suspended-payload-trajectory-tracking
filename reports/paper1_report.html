Phase 2 report · Paper 1 reproduction and stress test

Geometric control of a cable-suspended payload on a realistic quadrotor model

Haidar Saad · advisor Prof. Andry · 4 November 2026 · simulation in MuJoCo 3.17, Skydio X2 (MuJoCo Menagerie) · code: github.com/haidar996/suspended-payload-trajectory-tracking

Summary. We reimplemented Sreenath, Lee & Kumar (CDC 2013), the differential-flatness map and the geometric controller for a quadrotor carrying a point-mass load on a rigid massless cable, and ran it on a realistic simulated drone: the Skydio X2 with 0–13 N rotors. Under the paper's own assumptions every result reproduces (flatness to 1e-13 against an independent implementation, sub-millimetre tracking, recovery from a 178° flip). Each assumption we then removed broke something specific: rotor limits cap the attitude bandwidth and make large-angle recovery chaotic; a cable that can go slack makes the 178° recovery diverge; a 1 m cable crashes on a 15° initial swing with the published gain scaling; and mocap-level velocity noise crashes every run because the controller has no estimator. A 35 ms motor lag breaks swing recovery, and slowing the gains to tolerate it leaves the swing undamped. These are the gaps the next papers, and the planned contribution, address.

2. What matched Paper 1

  • Differential flatness. Exact derivatives to 6th order by jet arithmetic; equation-of-motion residual 1e-14; agreement with Udaan 1.1e-13; open-loop replay in MuJoCo within 1 cm for 3–6 s.
  • Controller modes. Attitude error decay 28.4 vs 27 /s predicted; cable decay 7.6 vs 6.75 /s; 1 m step settles in 2.87 s vs 2.83 s from linear design.
  • Tracking. Circle, figure-8 and point-to-point references tracked to ≤ 0.17 mm. Ablation: flatness feed-forward 0.05 mm RMSE, numerical differentiation 12.5 mm, none 0.67 m.
  • Fig. 3 trend. As the period T₀ of the load ellipse shrinks, the required drone motion becomes far more aggressive (below ≈ 1.25 s the drone must tilt past 90°).
  • Fig. 4–5 recovery. From a 178° flip of drone and cable, all errors converge to zero (inside 5 cm at 5.1 s), with unlimited thrust and a rigid cable.

3. What changes on a realistic drone

Each finding below removes one assumption of Paper 1. IDs refer to the project's design log (SIMULATION_DESIGN.md).

F3
Rotor limits cap the attitude bandwidth. With 3× loop separation the cascade is lightly damped; the paper's singular-perturbation argument needs ≈ 5×. At 40–60 rad/s attitude bandwidth a 30° swing saturates the X2 rotors and the drone diverges, so 30 rad/s is the ceiling and the outer loops follow at 7.5 and 1.5 rad/s.
F4
The X2 has a speed limit, and so does the controller. The shortest ellipse period the X2 rotors allow is T₀* = 2.12 s (0.5 × 1.0 m; load 3.0 m/s, 8.8 m/s²) and 2.61 s (1.0 × 2.0 m). Above it the X2 tracks exactly like an ideal actuator; slightly below, the mixer still copes; further below it crashes. At T₀ = 1.5 s even unlimited thrust fails: the reference needs ≈ 62 rad/s body rates, beyond the 30 rad/s attitude loop.
F5
Large-angle recovery is chaotic under rotor limits. The controller asks for 15–46 N m against ≈ 2 N m available. Flips of 0–10° recover cleanly; from 20° the outcome flips between success and failure with 1° changes (30° fails, 31° recovers), and recoveries lose 1–9 m of height.
F6
A slack cable in simulation. The C2 tendon reproduces C1 to 0.1 mm while taut, gives exactly zero tension and ballistic flight while slack. The peak tension at a snap is not physical for an inextensible cable (it is set by numerical softness); the snap impulse is, and it converges at 0.5 ms.
F7
Paper 1's recovery relies on the cable pushing. On C2, all taut benchmark cases match C1 to 0.03 mm, and a payload thrown upward at 3 m/s is recovered in 2.4 s. But the 178° recovery diverges even with unlimited thrust: with the load above the drone and the cable slack, thrust no longer acts on the load and the drone is launched ≈ 21 m in 0.1 s. Flip-sweep recoveries drop from 14/19 to 7/19.
F8
Bandwidth-based gains do not transfer across cable lengths. 0.25 m works everywhere. At 1.0 m the cable gain k_q ∝ l doubles the restoring force, and a 15° initial swing flips the X2 (30° fails even with unlimited thrust). The X2 speed limit also falls with length: T₀* = 1.94 / 2.12 / 2.26 s for 0.25 / 0.5 / 1.0 m.
F9
A body-rate interface drops the feed-forward torque. Paper 1's attitude law equals a P attitude loop inside a P rate loop exactly (1e-14 N m), except the Ω̇_c feed-forward, which a thrust-plus-rates command cannot carry: tracking error grows from 0.17 mm to 3–3.6 mm.
F10
Realistic sensing and actuation. One factor at a time, 990 runs, 20 seeds for noisy settings. Motor lag 35 ms breaks swing recovery only; delay, drag and the rate interface have small effects. Sensor noise crashes every run (220/220): the cause is velocity noise, because the controller forms the cable rate from two raw velocities and passes it through the 30 rad/s attitude loop. Gains slow enough for the lag (0.4 / 2 / 10 rad/s) never crash, but cannot damp a 30° swing.

4. Limits of this study

  • Noise is applied as independent white noise at every 500 Hz tick. Filtered motion-capture data is smoother, so F10's severity depends on this assumption; the qualitative need for an estimator does not.
  • The 35 ms motor time constant comes from another platform; the X2's is not identified. It is randomised in later robustness tests.
  • Gains were not re-tuned per condition, on purpose: the study tests Paper 1 as published. Systematic tuning follows the agreed protocol (D12) in Phase 3.
  • No rotor downwash on the payload and no ground effect, as in the reference papers.

5. Gaps for the next phases

  1. Actuator-aware control. A controller that respects 0–13 N per rotor instead of assuming unlimited thrust (F3, F4, F5).
  2. Slack-aware recovery. Handling the cable's pull-only nature, especially when the load is above the drone (F7).
  3. State estimation. Paper 3's Kalman filter is the natural next test against F10.
  4. Length-robust gains. One design that works from 0.25 m to 1.0 m without retuning (F8).
  5. Lag-compatible swing damping. Meeting the 35 ms motor lag and the payload swing frequency at the same time, which the cascade structure cannot do by retuning alone (F10).

Next: Paper 2 (survey) defines baselines and the tuning protocol; Paper 3 (LQR + Kalman filter, elastic cable C3) is then tested on the same benchmark.
